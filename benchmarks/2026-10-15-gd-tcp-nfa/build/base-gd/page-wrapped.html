Gradient Descent by Hand

One parameter, one loss, every number visible. Step through the updates yourself and watch what the learning rate does.

The setup

We want the value of w that makes the loss as small as possible. The loss is a parabola with its lowest point at w = 3:

L(w) = (w − 3)²    L′(w) = 2(w − 3)

The derivative L′(w) is the slope. It is negative left of 3 and positive right of 3. Gradient descent moves w a little in the direction opposite to the slope:

wk+1 = wk − η · L′(wk)
wk
the parameter after k steps. We start at w0 = 0.
η
the learning rate (eta). It scales how far each step goes.

First step with η = 0.3: the slope is L′(0) = 2(0 − 3) = −6. The step is 0.3 × (−6) = −1.8. So w₁ = 0 − (−1.8) = 1.8, and the loss drops from 9 to (1.8 − 3)² = 1.44.

Why this happens

Put the gradient into the update rule and subtract 3 from both sides:

wk+1 − 3 = (1 − 2η) · (wk − 3)

So every step multiplies the distance to the minimum by the same number, 1 − 2η. That one number explains every case:

0 < η < 0.5
The factor is between 0 and 1. w approaches 3 from one side. A small η gives a factor close to 1, so progress is slow. With η = 0.05 the factor is 0.9, and it takes 55 steps to get within 0.01 of the minimum.
η = 0.5
The factor is 0. One step lands exactly on 3. This only works because the loss is a perfect parabola.
0.5 < η < 1
The factor is between −1 and 0. w jumps over the minimum each step, but each jump is shorter. It still converges, with a zig-zag.
η = 1
The factor is −1. w bounces between 0 and 6 forever.
η > 1
The factor is less than −1. Each jump is longer than the last, so the loss grows without limit. With η = 1.05 the distance grows by 10% every step.

For a real model the loss is not a perfect parabola, and the safe range of η depends on how sharply the loss curves. The pattern is the same: too small wastes steps, too big makes training unstable.
