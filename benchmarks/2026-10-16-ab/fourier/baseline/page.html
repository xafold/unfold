Building a square wave out of sine waves

A square wave jumps between −1 and +1. It has sharp corners, and a sine wave has none. Fourier's claim is that you can still write the square wave as a sum of sines:

f(x)=4π(sin⁡x+sin⁡3x3+sin⁡5x5+⋯)

The figure adds the terms one at a time. On the left, each sine is a circle that turns: the term 4πksin⁡kx is a circle of radius 4πk that turns k times as fast as the first. Stack the circles end to end, and the height of the last point is the sum. On the right, that height is drawn against x.

Why only odd harmonics? The square wave has a symmetry: shift it by half a period and it flips sign, f(x+π)=−f(x). The odd sines sin⁡kx with odd k have the same symmetry. The even ones do not, so their coefficients are 0.

Why amplitude 1/k? The coefficient is bk=1π∫−ππf(x)sin⁡kxdx=4πk for odd k. A jump in the function makes the coefficients fall only as fast as 1/k, so many terms are needed for sharp corners.

The overshoot does not go away. More terms push the error into a thinner and thinner band next to each jump. But the highest peak of the sum does not go down to 1. It goes to 2π∫0πsin⁡ttdt≈1.179, about 9% of the jump. This is the Gibbs phenomenon. The sum still converges at every point that is not a jump, and the RMS error goes to 0.
